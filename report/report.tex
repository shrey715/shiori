\section*{Setup}

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.2cm}Y@{}}
\textbf{Document} & The ICC T20 World Cup 2026 playing conditions. I picked them because they are full of ``unless / except / shall not'' clauses, and the appendices (DRS, Super Over, warm-up matches, reserve day) override the main rules. The PDF is from February 2026, so the LLM can't have seen it. \\
\textbf{Chunking} & Split by clause number (e.g.\ 9.1, 9.1.1), consecutive clauses of a section merged up to $\sim$180 tokens; headers, diagram labels and blank forms dropped. \textbf{440 chunks}, kept small on purpose: with big chunks the answer is almost always in the top 3 already. Every chunk exists as \textbf{A} (plain text) and \textbf{B} (section title + text). \\
\textbf{Models} & Bi-encoders: \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}, \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5} (FAISS, exact cosine). Re-rankers: \texttt{ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2} (used for Answer 2), \texttt{bge-reranker-base} (rankings only). LLM: \texttt{Qwen3-32B-AWQ} on vLLM over 4$\times$RTX 2080 Ti, temperature 0. \\
\textbf{Questions} & 40 questions in 4 tiers of 10, each harder than the last: direct wording, fan paraphrase, exceptions/negations, traps/cross-references. Each has a gold chunk and an expected answer, checked against the rule text. \\
\textbf{Grading} & Every answer (No RAG, Answer 1, Answer 2) graded against the expected answer as correct (1), partial (0.5), wrong or abstain (0), by the same LLM with a fixed rubric. A blind second grader (Claude Haiku) agreed exactly on 79\% and within one step on 97\%; I fixed the 5 clear grader errors by hand. \\
\end{tabularx}

\section{Bi-encoder vs.\ Cross-encoder}

Both of my models use the \emph{same} backbone: a 6-layer MiniLM transformer (384 dims, 12 attention heads). The difference is only in \textbf{how the question and the chunk are fed in} (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}), and that changes everything about cost and accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:arch}).

\begin{align*}
\text{bi-encoder: } & s(q,d) = \cos\big(E(q),\,E(d)\big), \quad E(x) = \text{mean-pool}\big(\text{MiniLM}(x)\big) \\
\text{cross-encoder: } & s(q,d) = \mathbf{w}^\top\, \mathbf{h}_{\texttt{[CLS]}}\big(\text{MiniLM}(\texttt{[CLS]}\ q\ \texttt{[SEP]}\ d\ \texttt{[SEP]})\big)
\end{align*}

\centering\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{2.7cm}YY@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{aspect} & \textbf{Bi-encoder} & \textbf{Cross-encoder} \\
\midrule
input & question and chunk encoded \emph{separately} & question and chunk joined into \emph{one} sequence \\
interaction & none until the final dot product (``late'') & in every layer, each question token attends to each chunk token (``early'') \\
output & two 384-d vectors, compared by cosine & one relevance score \\
precompute & all chunk vectors, once & nothing, the score depends on the question \\
cost per query & 1 encoder pass + $N$ dot products & $N$ encoder passes \\
trained to & put sentences with similar meaning close together & judge whether this passage answers this query (MS MARCO labels) \\

\bottomrule

\end{table}

\textbf{Why it matters, with a real example (q35).} For ``In a 7-overs-a-side rain game, what's the most a single bowler can send down?'' the bi-encoder ranked clause 13.9.1 (``No bowler shall bowl more than 4 overs'') first, cosine 0.575, and the actual answer, 13.9.2.2 (``between 5 and 9 overs \ldots{} no more than two overs''), fourth, cosine 0.519. Both chunks are about overs per bowler, so their vectors sit close to the question; the single vector has no room for the ``5 to 9 overs'' condition. The cross-encoder reads ``7-overs'' next to ``between 5 and 9 overs'' in the same attention pass and flips them: 13.9.2.2 scores $-3.34$ (\#1) vs.\ $-4.74$ for 13.9.1. Same model size, but because the two texts meet inside the network, it can check the condition instead of just the topic.

So the baseline failed in exactly the ways the assignment describes: it \textbf{left out an exception} (q23, q38), \textbf{applied the wrong rule} (q35), \textbf{made something up} (q36), or gave up because the right clause wasn't there (q28). In 12 of the 16 rank fixes the answer improved too.

\subsection{Where re-ranking failed}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Regressions.} The cross-encoder can also be fooled by surface matches. For q22 (``umpire calls a wide, keeper whips the bails off'') it ranked helmet and fielder clauses first, pushing the right one (22.9) from \#2 to \#5, and the answer went from correct to wrong. For q33 (``can the same bowler bowl the next Super Over?'') a clause about the ``same bowler'' offending a third time (41.13.4) beat the Super Over rule (\#3 $\to$ \#7).
  \item \textbf{Lost chunks.} For 5 questions the gold chunk was below rank 10 in every setup, so the re-ranker never saw it. E.g.\ for ``Is there a follow-on in T20?'' the chunk just says ``Shall not apply.'' and shares no words with the question.
  \item \textbf{Reading failures.} For q24, q31 and q40 the gold chunk was \#1 and the answer was still wrong: the LLM read ``However, \ldots{} they shall refuse'' as the rule (q24), ``complete or replay'' as ``replay'' (q40), and ignored ``no more than 11 may bat'' (q31). Retrieval can't fix that.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Is it just the LLM's own knowledge?}
No. Without the document the LLM is \textbf{wrong 55\% of the time} and almost never says it doesn't know. Splitting the questions into general cricket law vs.\ tournament-specific rules makes it clear (Figure~\ref{fig:scope}): the model knows general laws (0.53) but not this tournament's rules (0.20). On those, RAG takes it to 0.64, and re-ranking to \textbf{0.73}. On general laws re-ranking slightly hurt (0.67 $\to$ 0.63), mostly because of the q22 regression.

In absolute terms that's cheap, and small next to the LLM call (seconds), so for these accuracy gains it's clearly worth it. Batching queries brings Pipeline 2 down to 7 ms each.

\section{Scaling: why not cross-encode everything?}

Because the cost grows with the number of chunks, and nothing can be precomputed. A cross-encoder needs the question, so for every new query it has to run one forward pass per chunk. Here, scoring all 440 chunks took \textbf{295 ms per query}, about 6{,}000$\times$ slower than the FAISS search (0.05 ms). That's $\sim$0.67 ms per chunk, so a collection of 1M chunks would take about \textbf{11 minutes per query} on this GPU. Bi-encoder chunk embeddings are computed once, offline, and vector search (especially approximate indexes) stays in milliseconds even for millions of chunks.

So the two-stage setup gets most of the benefit for a fraction of the cost: the cheap bi-encoder narrows millions of chunks to 10, and the expensive cross-encoder only has to sort those 10. The trade-off, which my lost cases show, is that re-ranking can only fix what the first stage retrieves. A chunk that the bi-encoder ranks 100th stays lost.
